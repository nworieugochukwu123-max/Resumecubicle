01

Write a CV summary that earns the next read

The summary at the top of your CV is often the only part read in full. Keep it to three or four lines and make every word count.

A simple formula

  • Who you are: your title or field, and how long you’ve worked in it
  • What you’re best at: two or three strengths that match the role
  • What you want next: the kind of role you’re aiming for
Customer service professional with five years’ experience in retail and call-centre teams. Skilled at resolving complaints quickly and training new colleagues. Looking for a team-leader role in a customer-focused business.

Skip phrases like “hard-working team player”. Everyone says them, so they tell the reader nothing.

02

Turn duties into results

A list of duties tells an employer what the job was. Results tell them what you did with it. For each role, pick your three or four strongest points and write them as achievements.

Try this pattern

Start with an action verb, say what you did, then say what changed.

Reorganised the stockroom layout, cutting average picking time by a quarter.

No numbers? Use another measure

  • Scale: how many people, customers, orders or locations
  • Frequency: daily, weekly, every shift
  • Speed: how much faster or sooner something was done
  • Recognition: praise, awards or extra responsibility you were given

If you’re estimating, be honest about it. “Around 30 customers a day” is better than an exact figure you can’t support.

03

Structure a cover letter that gets read

Keep your cover letter to one page. Four short paragraphs are enough.

  • The opening: name the role and say, in one or two sentences, why you’re a strong match
  • The evidence: one or two achievements that relate directly to the job description
  • The fit: why this employer and this role interest you, with something specific to them
  • The close: thank the reader and say you’d welcome a conversation

Write to a named person when you can. Avoid repeating your CV line by line. The letter’s job is to connect your experience to what this employer needs.

04

Make your LinkedIn profile easy to find

Recruiters search LinkedIn with keywords, so the words in your profile decide whether you appear.

  • Headline: go beyond your job title. Include your field, key skills and what you offer
  • About section: write a short, readable summary in the first person, with your strengths up front
  • Experience: mirror your CV, and use the same job titles and dates so nothing contradicts
  • Skills: list the ones that appear in job descriptions you’re targeting
  • Photo and contact details: use a clear, friendly photo and make it easy to reach you

Check that your profile and CV agree. A recruiter who notices a mismatch will usually trust neither.

05

Build a job search routine that lasts

A search can take time, and steady effort beats occasional bursts. A simple weekly rhythm keeps you moving without burning out.

  • Set a target list: roles and employers you would genuinely like to work for
  • Tailor each application: adjust your CV and letter to the job description, even slightly
  • Track everything: note where you applied, when, and when to follow up
  • Follow up: a short, polite message after a week or two can bring your application back to the top
  • Talk to people: let friends, former colleagues and contacts know what you’re looking for
  • Review weekly: notice what is getting replies, and do more of it
06

CV mistakes worth fixing today

These are small to fix and make a real difference to how your CV is read.

  • Vague bullet points that describe duties but not results
  • Spelling and grammar slips. Read it aloud, or ask someone else to read it
  • Dense blocks of text with no breathing room
  • Outdated or missing contact details
  • Sending the same CV to every employer
  • Inconsistent dates, fonts or formatting
  • Including old or irrelevant experience at the same length as recent work
